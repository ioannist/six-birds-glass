\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{The problem}

Anderson called the nature of glass and the glass transition probably the deepest and most
interesting unsolved problem in solid-state theory \cite{anderson-1995}. One face of that problem
is sixty years old and unusually concrete: no universally accepted static order parameter for
glass has emerged. A glass and a liquid can agree in density, energy, and every routinely
monitored one-time observable, yet behave differently \cite{debenedetti-stillinger-2001}.
Conventional low-order static correlations show no clear signature of the transition, and the
candidate structural measures that do grow remain contested
\cite{berthier-biroli-2011,cavagna-2009,tarjus-2011,royall-williams-2015}.

A second face has sat in the laboratory record since 1963: Kovacs's crossover experiment
\cite{kovacs-1963}. Equilibrate a polymer glass hot, quench it, age it, then jump the temperature
up and watch its volume relax. At some moment the volume \emph{equals} its new equilibrium value,
and it does not stay there: it overshoots through a non-monotone hump before returning. At the
crossing moment the sample is indistinguishable from equilibrium in the monitored observable and
distinguishable from it in its future, a point made explicit at the level of configurations in
simulation \cite{mossa-sciortino-2004}. The standard partial fix, the
Tool--Narayanaswamy--Moynihan (TNM) fictive temperature
\cite{tool-1946,narayanaswamy-1971,moynihan-1976}, adds one memory variable. Richer protocols
expose the limits of any single such variable and have motivated multiparameter models
\cite{ritland-1956,kahr-1979,hodge-1994,mckenna-simon-2017}. Memory effects of this kind, in
which a material's response depends on its history in a way that no monitored one-time
observable records, are now studied across many classes of matter \cite{keim-2019}.

\subsection{The question, made precise}

Both faces ask the same question. Take a declared readout of the system (here, the energy of a
spin chain; in the laboratory, the volume) and ask: \emph{is that readout a closed description of
the dynamics?} A readout is closed over a horizon $\tau$ if its present value determines the
statistics of its value $\tau$ steps later. Then a law can be written in the readout's vocabulary
without loss; this is the informational closure of \cite{bertschinger-2006,pfante-2014}. In
Markov-chain language the readout is closed at horizon $\tau$ exactly when the $\tau$-step
readout transition probabilities agree across every populated microstate with the same readout.
For a fully supported distribution this is lumpability of the readout
\cite{burke-rosenblatt-1958,kemeny-snell-1960}. The Kovacs hump is a direct demonstration that the
monitored volume is not closed.

We use ``static order parameter'' in one operational sense only: a function of the declared
readout that separates two preparations whose futures differ. A function of the readout that
fails to separate them (a constant does so trivially) cannot carry the information the futures
carry. That is narrower than the field's usage, and it is the only sense in which this paper
speaks of foreclosing the search.

Put this way, three further questions become computable on a finite model. How far from closed is
the readout, in nats? Can one exhibit a concrete certificate of the failure, two preparation
histories that agree on the readout now and differ in their futures? And can \emph{any} function
of the readout recover the missing distinction, or must a genuinely new coordinate carry it, and
at what cost?

\subsection{What this paper does}

We answer these questions on small finite Markov models. The kinetically constrained East model
\cite{jackle-eisinger-1991,ritort-sollich-2003,garrahan-sollich-toninelli-2011} is the substrate
on which everything is built and calibrated; the kinetically constrained Fredrickson--Andersen
model \cite{fredrickson-andersen-1984} and a Bouchaud-type trap family
\cite{bouchaud-1992,monthus-bouchaud-1996} are out-of-sample transfer targets. At the sizes used
($N \le 10$ spins for certificates) the transition kernels, stationary measures, witness pairs
and zero controls are ratios of integers. Where a certificate needs logarithms, as the closure
deficit does, every inequality is proved with rational interval enclosures. The price is
that every result is a statement about a declared finite class of models, sizes, observables and
protocols, and we state that class in every claim.

The bookkeeping that keeps those scopes honest comes from a certificate-based closure calculus,
Six Birds Theory (SBT) \sbtcite{sbt-primer,sbt-foundations-i,sbt-foundations-ii,%
sbt-foundations-iii,sbt-foundations-iv,sbt-paper-a,sbt-paper-b,sbt-paper-c}. The reader does not
need that corpus. \S\ref{sec:methods} defines every object in ordinary probabilistic terms, and the
framework's labels appear only where a claim is filed in its vocabulary, always with a plain
gloss.

In more detail:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{GT1, the energy readout is not closed.} The closure deficit is the conditional
    mutual information between the microstate now and the energy later, given the energy now.
    For an aging East chain it exceeds the same quantity at constrained equilibrium by a factor of
    at least $3/2$ at every declared horizon. The equilibrium value is not zero: closure depends
    on where the mobile excitations sit, not on whether the chain is stationary.
  \item \textbf{GT2, the Kovacs witness.} The Kovacs protocol on East yields an exact pair of
    preparation histories with the same present mean energy and different future mean energies
    under the declared continuation.
  \item \textbf{GT3, the order-parameter search is foreclosed on the declared class.} Each of the
    $512$ yes/no functions of the energy agrees on a constructed witness pair (indeed the two
    histories have the same complete energy distribution) that a finer readout of the same
    microstate, the occupation of the site next to the wall, separates. Whatever separates the
    pair must carry information the energy readout does not.
  \item \textbf{GT4, the kill-test panel.} The classifier that accepts the Kovacs memory
    candidate rejects five schematic look-alikes: a protocol artifact, dissipation, an
    explicitly latent variable, an equilibrium control, and an incomplete continuation catalog.
  \item \textbf{GT5, the cost of repair.} One added scalar coordinate resolves the GT2 pair. A
    fictive-temperature-like index (the index of the grid point whose equilibrium mean energy is
    closest to each microstate's energy, averaged over the history) is one of three that do. This
    shows the repair mechanism; it does not prove that one scalar suffices, or fails, in general.
  \item \textbf{GT6, the memory layer is strictly richer than the full energy law.} GT3's pair
    is constructed and separated by a present readout. GT6 asks the same of real aging: on a
    frozen aging shell, two lawful randomized stops of one run have \emph{identical} current
    energy distributions, exactly, and the East dynamics drives their mean energies apart at every
    horizon from $1$ to $21$. So the
    future-equivalence description of histories is not a function of the current energy law,
    and no descriptor computed from that law, the fictive-temperature-like index included, is
    future-sufficient there. The gaps are tiny (about $10^{-10}$) but exact. The framework's
    fuller strict-extension filing, which also asks for multiple stable fixed points of a
    packaging map, does not pass on this shell, and we show why.
  \item \textbf{GT7, aging constants transfer only partly.} Six aging readouts were frozen on
    East and interval predictions for FA and the trap family were registered and hash-pinned. The
    targets were run and scored mechanically: 8 of 22 scored predictions passed, all 14 failures
    lie on the same side, and the trap family produced no Kovacs hump at all, for a reason we
    prove.
\end{itemize}

Two further results come from the formalization (\S\ref{sec:lean}). In the Lean proof assistant
the abstract theorems are machine-checked, together with the exact witness tables of GT2 and GT6
as exported from the computation. And the legacy formal core we started from provably cannot
express any witness at all; the theorem locates exactly which axiom causes the collapse.

\subsection{How to read the claims}

We would defend four contributions, in this order. First, the \textbf{discipline}: every claim
quantifies over a declared finite class, is pinned by content hash to the artifact that supports
it, comes with controls, and is filed together with what it does \emph{not} assert. Honest
negatives are results. Second, the \textbf{result complex} itself: the claims, bridges and honest
negatives of Table~\ref{tab:at-a-glance}, each hash-pinned to its artifacts. Third, the
\textbf{freeze-and-transfer protocol with its actual scoreboard}: the failures are results, with a
clean one-sided pattern and a proven structural explanation for the empty column. Fourth, the
\textbf{Lean-checked core and its scope-boundary theorem}.

What this paper is not: it proves nothing about all glasses, the continuum, or the ideal-glass
transition; it makes no irreversibility claims; and its field-facing sentence, ``no static order
parameter,'' is licensed only over the declared class on which it is proved. The full list of
nonclaims is printed, unabridged, in Appendix~\ref{app:nonclaims}.
